# Go-to-Market - Miru Digital

## 1. Market Context

Miru Digital sits between solo tabletop RPGs, indie narrative games, and digital play aids. The opportunity is not mass-market mobile RPG scale. It is a focused audience of players who are already adjacent to solo play but need the first session to feel less like setup work. That audience is visible in solo RPG communities, itch.io circles, indie game newsletters, actual-play-adjacent spaces, and small Discord communities built around journaling games and rules-light RPGs.

The timing is good because solo RPGs have become more legible to digital-native players, while AI coding tools make a faithful, rules-aware digital adaptation feasible for a solo founder. The constraint is rights. The GTM plan should assume a private prototype first, then a creator/publisher conversation, then a public launch only if permission and positioning are clear.

## 2. Launch Strategy

Pre-launch should be rights-first and prototype-led. The goal is not to build a public audience before permission exists; the goal is to create enough of the product to demonstrate care, fidelity, and restraint to the Miru creator or publisher. The pre-launch artifact is a polished private prototype showing the Next Day magic moment, source verification discipline, and mobile-first play table.

Soft launch should be a controlled private alpha with 10-20 people after rights status is clear enough to permit testing. The best testers are split between digital-native indie players who have not played Miru and experienced solo RPG players who can judge rules feel. This release should be invitation-only, feedback-heavy, and framed as product validation rather than promotion.

Public launch should happen only after one of three decisions: Miru Digital is authorized, it becomes an official/partnered digital adaptation, or it is renamed and repositioned as an inspired-by original project with source-specific content removed. If authorized, public launch should emphasize "guided way to play Miru" and show the play table in action. If renamed, messaging must shift to the original game loop and avoid Miru branding.

## 3. Pre-Launch Playbook

**Week -8: Prepare the rights pitch.** Build a one-page creator brief: what Miru Digital is, what it is not, how it protects the analog feel, what source material is used, what would be public, and what rights or partnership model you are asking about. Include two screenshots or mockups only if they already communicate restraint.

**Week -7: Finish the private prototype spine.** Record a 60-90 second screen capture of the core play table: start run, tap Next Day, roll, resolve state, camp, journal. Do not post this publicly yet if rights are unresolved. Use it as a private artifact.

**Week -6: Contact the creator or publisher.** Send a short, respectful email with the one-page brief and prototype clip. Ask for the right person and the preferred way to discuss a digital adaptation. The ask should be specific: permission to continue a private prototype, permission to run limited testing, and conditions for public release.

**Week -5: Build the tester list quietly.** Identify 20-30 possible testers without making a public launch claim. Sources: personal network, solo RPG Discords where you are already welcome, indie game friends, and players who have bounced off PDFs. Keep a simple spreadsheet with name, audience type, contact, device, and consent status.

**Week -4: Create feedback instruments.** Prepare a 20-minute test script, a post-session survey, and a bug report template. The key questions: Did you know what to do next? Did the app feel like a guide or a video game? Where did you stop trusting it? Would you continue the run?

**Week -3: Run 3 internal dry runs.** Play through the app yourself on a real phone, then have 2 trusted people do the same over screen share. Fix blocking onboarding, save, map, and prompt issues before inviting broader testers.

**Week -2: Invite first private testers.** Invite 5 people only. Ask them to complete the first three in-game days and record friction. Do not explain the rules manually unless they are blocked; the point is to test the product.

**Week -1: Expand to 10-20 testers.** Invite a balanced group: half digital-native indie players and half solo RPG hobbyists. Ask for one session within 7 days. Offer a short thank-you note and, if appropriate, future credit in acknowledgments.

## 4. Launch Week Plan

**Day 1: Private alpha opens.** Send tester invitations with a clear scope: private prototype, do not redistribute, rights status if relevant, and target task: complete at least three in-game days. Watch activation, first camp completion, and save errors.

**Day 2: Observe sessions.** Run 2-3 live or recorded playtests. Take notes on hesitation points, not just verbal feedback. The most important moments are first run creation, first Next Day tap, first rules prompt, first camp, and first resume.

**Day 3: Fix blockers only.** Address P0 issues that prevent play or break trust: auth failure, save loss, invalid action, unreadable mobile layout, or wrong rule flow. Do not add new features.

**Day 4: Ask for qualitative reactions.** Send a short survey: "What did you expect to be harder?", "Where did the app help?", "Where did it take over too much?", "Would you play again tomorrow?", "What would you tell a friend this is?"

**Day 5: Run hobbyist review.** Ask 2-3 solo RPG players to focus on fidelity and tone. They should compare the feel to analog play and identify where the app flattens interpretation or hides too much.

**Day 6: Compile the rights/update package.** Summarize tester outcomes, known issues, and next plan. If creator/publisher conversation is active, send an update showing care and progress.

**Day 7: Decide next release gate.** Choose one: continue private alpha, prepare public authorized launch, reposition/rename, or pause until rights are clear. The decision should be based on rights status, activation data, and rules fidelity confidence.

## 5. Post-Launch Growth

Weeks 1-2 after soft launch should focus on activation and trust. Fix the points where players fail to complete first camp, fail to understand the current prompt, or fail to resume. Do not chase acquisition until activation is working.

Weeks 3-4 should add the highest-impact rules completeness gaps and source verification fixes. Publish no public content unless rights allow it. If public sharing is permitted, share one progress clip per week showing a concrete product improvement, such as better map resume context or cleaner camp flow.

Weeks 5-8 should expand tester volume to 30-50 people if the product is stable. Segment feedback by audience. Digital-native players should be judged on onboarding and confidence. Solo RPG hobbyists should be judged on fidelity and whether the app preserves authorship.

Weeks 9-12 should prepare the chosen launch path. If authorized, build a small public page, a concise press kit, and a Product Hunt or itch.io launch plan. If not authorized, remove or replace source-specific content and turn the work into an original solo RPG engine or private portfolio prototype.

## 6. Channel Strategy

**1. Creator/publisher relationship - highest ROI, highest dependency.** This is the gating channel. A respectful rights conversation can unlock legitimacy, source access, promotion, and permission. Effort is medium; return is decisive; timeline is unpredictable.

**2. Private tester network - highest learning ROI.** Recruit through personal contacts and small communities. Effort is low to medium; return is high-quality product feedback; timeline is immediate once a prototype exists.

**3. Solo RPG communities - strong but sensitive.** Reddit communities, Discords, itch.io circles, and forums can be valuable, but only if you participate respectfully and avoid drive-by promotion. Effort is medium; return is credibility and testers; timeline is 4-8 weeks.

**4. Indie game social posts - useful after rights clarity.** Short clips on Bluesky, Mastodon, X, or Threads can show the magic moment. Effort is low per post but consistency matters; return is awareness; timeline is 6-12 weeks.

**5. Product Hunt or broad launch platforms - later only.** These channels are poor for rights-unclear private prototypes. Use them only if there is an authorized public launch and the product is stable enough for broad attention.

## 7. Content Strategy

Before rights clarity, create private artifacts rather than public hype: prototype clips, a creator brief, tester guide, playtest script, source verification notes, and a short product narrative. These assets support conversations without making public claims.

After rights clarity, publish concrete product progress 2-3 times per week for 4-6 weeks before launch. Good post topics: "How a solo RPG turn becomes a guided mobile flow," "Designing a play table that does not replace imagination," "What we learned from first-camp completion tests," and "Why resume context matters for solo RPGs."

The strongest content format is short video or GIF with captions. Show the map, dice, current prompt, and state change. Avoid long theory posts until people can see the product working.

## 8. Community Strategy

Show up first as a careful builder and player, not a marketer. The relevant communities value craft, respect for creators, and love of the analog form. Join conversations about solo RPG onboarding, journaling friction, digital aids, and source fidelity. Do not lead with links.

The best early ask is narrow: "I am building a private guided prototype for a solo RPG and need feedback on whether it preserves authorship. Would you try a 20-minute session?" That invites useful criticism rather than passive signups.

Do not create a dedicated Miru Digital community until there is a public path. A small tester email list or private Discord channel is enough for alpha feedback. Community ownership too early becomes another product to maintain.

## 9. Key Metrics

Acquisition target for private alpha: 20 invited testers, 15 accepted, 10 completed first session. Public acquisition targets should wait until rights are clear.

Activation target: 50% of invited testers complete first camp without help. Great is 70%. Track time to first Next Day, time to first camp, and number of rule lookups opened.

Retention target: 25% of testers return within 7 days to continue a run. Great is 40%. Track resume success, latest day reached, and whether the player adds more than one journal entry.

Quality target: zero known P0 source-rule mismatches in the tested path, fewer than two P1 confusion points per session, and 99% autosave success.

Revenue target: none. The product is free during rights-first validation. If monetization becomes relevant later, revisit after public rights and retention are proven.

## 10. Budget Considerations

Keep early spend low. Use Vercel and Supabase free tiers if possible. Budget for a domain only after launch path is clear. The highest-value investment is not ads; it is time spent on source verification, mobile QA, tester observation, and a respectful rights pitch.

Expected early monthly spend: $0-25 for hosted infrastructure, $0-20 for domain/email tooling if needed, and optional $20-50 for transcription, design, or testing utilities. Avoid paid acquisition. A product this niche should earn its first users through community trust, creator relationship, and visible craft.

If money is available, spend it on legal/rights clarity before marketing. A small paid consultation or formal agreement can save far more than it costs if the project becomes public.

## 11. Risks

**Rights ambiguity blocks public launch.** Mitigation: contact the creator or publisher early, keep early testing private, and prepare a renamed/inspired-by fallback if permission is not available.

**The audience is too niche for broad launch tactics.** Mitigation: optimize for depth, not volume. Ten serious testers are more useful than a thousand unqualified clicks.

**Community perceives the app as replacing analog play.** Mitigation: message it as a guided play table, not a video game adaptation, and show how journaling and interpretation remain with the player.

**Rules fidelity issues damage trust.** Mitigation: publish or share source verification discipline, track open issues, and avoid claiming rules completeness until tests and visual verification support it.

**The product looks too polished in the wrong way.** Mitigation: keep visual direction grounded in analog field kit cues and avoid reward spectacle in clips or copy.

**Gerg's time becomes the bottleneck.** Mitigation: keep the launch path narrow, use tester batches, and delay public content until the product can absorb attention.
